I had the pleasure of visiting Rome briefly last Summer. Rome is a feast for the eyes, from the remains of ancient temples and stadiums to majestic renaissance churches and baroque palaces. Standing on Rome's varied topography and amid its ancient ruins, something caught my fascination - Its Metro. A System that's been under continuous, incremental construction for decades with old and new sections coexisting and yet somehow threads through hills, ruins and centuries of buried history. The many pasts of Rome, grafted onto each other in myriad layers of history, have thus far prevented all attempts to make the city into an interlocking system. Over the centuries, Rome has refused to be tamed. The subway line is the most latest challenge to the city's obstinate individualism. Intrigued by its construction, I dug deeper and found parallels with my limited experience with legacy codebases.

Building a new subway line under any city is a challenge, but to build one under the historic center of Rome comes with some unique challenges. In a city 2,500 years old, the sights we see are merely the tip of the iceberg; beneath them lie layer upon layer of buried buildings, streets, and forgotten remnants of the ancient city. Which is why, unlike other metro networks around the world, Rome can't simply survey once and build. Every dig risks hitting something undocumented - a Roman barracks, a burial ground, a wall nobody mapped. One such finding was the [Hadrian's Athenaeum](https://www.thehistoryblog.com/archives/22476) which is currently open for public was found while digging Line C in 2009. Similarly, In 2016, A [2nd-century Roman military barracks](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-36311156) was unearthed during excavation for a metro station. These are not isolated discoveries, but quite the contrary. What we see today is only a fraction of ancient Rome; some estimates suggest that [barely 10% of the ancient city](https://formaurbis.stanford.edu/) has been excavated, leaving much of it buried beneath the modern city.

Faced with that kind of uncertainty, The instinct is to call this pathfinding[^astar] around obstacles. But there's a catch : the obstacles aren't known upfront. That's exactly what **code archaeology** feels like : touching an undocumented system where you don't know what depends on the thing you're about to change. 

The engineers of Rome's metro couldn't simply drill a few holes and start tunnelling. Before laying a single track, they first had to figure out what was hiding beneath the streets. Through preliminary soundings, using boreholes and small archaeological trenches, they got a glimpse of what lay beneath. But even these early glimpses revealed far more history than expected, forcing engineers to rethink how they'd build the metro. Touching a legacy system demands the same discipline. You don’t start by changing the code; you start by understanding it : tracing what calls what, stepping through the debugger, and digging through old commits to understand why something was built the way it was. Skip that, and you’re essentially tunnelling blind.

The tunnels for the subway line itself sit deep, about 90 feet below street level, but it's the stations and air vents that do most of the damage to antique artifacts, since it's shallower excavation that stands the best chance of running straight into a wall nobody mapped. This has forced multiple stations to be cancelled or shifted from their original locations. The Largo Argentina stop was scrapped entirely, one that would have served key tourist sites like the Pantheon, because what was found underground made the original plan untenable. Legacy systems have their own version of Largo Argentina, except you rarely find out until after you've already dug. I once changed a column's default value in a database, a small, contained-looking change with nothing pointing to it mattering beyond the table itself. Three systems away, a dashboard built by a team that no longer existed started quietly producing wrong numbers. Not an error, not a crash, nothing to notice, just numbers that were wrong. It took a month before anyone caught it, and by then nobody could say with certainty how many decisions had been made off that dashboard in the meantime. The distance between where I made the change and where it actually broke was the real lesson. I wasn't digging near the Pantheon. I was just changing a default value.

At Porta Metronia, excavation uncovered a remarkably well-preserved Roman military complex from the 2nd century AD, nearly nine metres beneath the modern city, with more than 30 rooms, mosaics, and frescoes. The discovery meant the original station design had to be rethought rather than simply building over the ruins. The ancient structures were carefully documented, temporarily relocated, restored, and eventually reconstructed inside the station in their original position and at their original levels. Today, the station incorporates the ruins into a museum space, allowing commuters to see a piece of ancient Rome as part of their journey. Programmers, in their hearts, are architects, and the first instinct on encountering something old and inconvenient is to bulldoze it and build something grand in its place. Rome's engineers didn't have that option, and the result is often better for the constraint: instead of a demolition, Porta Metronia became a station built *around* what it found, old and new occupying the same structure. The [Strangler Fig pattern](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/StranglerFigApplication.html) works the same way. New functionality grows around a legacy component instead of replacing it outright, calling into the old code where it still works, until eventually nothing needs the old path directly and it can be quietly removed. The barracks at Porta Metronia are still there. So, sometimes, is the code.

As the metro tunnels approached the Colosseum, engineers had to make sure the excavation didn't damage one of Rome's most fragile monuments. Sensors were installed around it to continuously track even tiny movements and vibrations caused by tunnelling, feeding back data that let engineers catch unexpected changes and adjust construction methods before any real damage was done. In a city where ancient structures sit just metres from modern infrastructure, watching became as important as construction itself. Legacy systems ask for the same discipline. You don't add a metric before a risky change and call it done, you watch the system the entire time you're operating near it, the same way those sensors never stopped reading once tunnelling began. A dashboard checked once before deploying tells you nothing about the hour after. The sensors near the Colosseum weren't there to approve the plan; they were there for as long as the tunnelling was.

The tunnels themselves run deep, about 150 feet below street level near the Colosseum, considerably deeper than they'd need to be if depth were the only concern. The extra depth exists for one reason: to keep vibration from reaching a structure nearly two thousand years old. Going deeper costs more, takes longer, and adds complexity to construction, but it was the price of not disturbing something too fragile to risk. Working with legacy systems sometimes calls for the same trade. The cheapest path is rarely the safest one. On one occasion, rather than modify a shared reporting utility that half a dozen dashboards quietly depended on, we built a new, parallel calculation for the one report that needed it, and left the old function untouched[^isolation], an instinct that turned out to already have a name : [Sprout Method](https://cavallini.ca/notes/2024-10-02-sprout-method-legacy-code/?utm_source=chatgpt.com). It meant more code, some duplication, and a slightly awkward seam between the two, but the alternative was touching something we couldn't fully see the edges of. Depth, in both cases, is what you pay to leave something alone.

But the parallel has a limit. Rome gets to preserve what it finds as part of the finished station. The barracks at Porta Metronia become a feature, not a compromise. Legacy code rarely gets that luxury. You can’t keep the old system around forever like a museum piece; sooner or later, something has to be replaced. The real difference is what “done” looks like. For Rome, it means making room for the old. For us, it often means finally letting it go.

Sometimes, the best thing you can do with a legacy system is let it go. It may be outdated, unreliable, insecure, or simply no longer suited to what the business needs today. Replacing it can open the door to newer technology, better performance and security, and lower maintenance costs and risks. However, replacing it can be costly as well as risky, so the trade-offs need to be weighed carefully.

When I first started working as a software developer, it was on a Chrome plugin that recorded browser actions and replayed them as automation scripts, built on a legacy jQuery codebase that had been mostly untouched for years. Everyone on the team hated everything about it, and at some point the team decided to replace it with something brand new. As a newbie, I was tasked with fixing bugs and adding features. 

The most valuable advice I ever received was to break out the debugger and start stepping through the code. A software system is the combination of the logic of the code and the data that flows through it, and a debugger is the best way to see both in action. I traced how a recorded click actually became a line of generated script, why certain DOM selectors were chosen over others, what the jQuery event bindings were quietly assuming about the page underneath them. None of it was documented. All of it was there if I was willing to step through it, and with time, I understood that codebase well enough to rebuild parts of it with real confidence. The plugin now generates automation scripts at around 75% accuracy, used across multiple QA teams. I didn't get there by reading the jQuery from the top down. I got there the way Rome's engineers understood what was beneath their streets: not by assuming, but by going down and looking.

In the age of AI, The desire to build and ship fast is inevitable but the confidence to build fast comes from assuming you know what's underground. Rome's engineers never had that luxury. Neither did I. I just decided to go find out what was actually down there before I touched anything.

[^astar]: A* is the traditional pathfinding in a maze where the entire maze/graph is known upfront before you start searching. The algorithm's job is just to find the optimal route through the grid that never changes dynamically. Rome's Metro is not that. The obstacles aren't known in advance. You don't find out a Roman barracks or a burial ground is sitting in your planned tunnel path until you're already digging. So this isn't a "find the best path through a known maze" problem but rather "find a path while the maze reveals new walls as you go, sometimes forcing you to backtrack, redesign, or reroute mid-execution."

[^isolation]: Isolating legacy systems can be seen as a form of software "quarantine," preventing the spread of technical debt and design flaws to newer, cleaner systems. This isolation isn't merely a technical barrier but can also represent a psychological and organizational boundary. It delineates the old from the new, the problematic from the well-designed, and in doing so, it can shift the organizational mindset. This demarcation encourages a culture of continuous improvement and adherence to modern best practices in the newly developed systems, while also acknowledging the existence and containment of past mistakes within the legacy system.
